*Cool-down is the two weeks after each cycle with nothing scheduled. People fix what they want, explore, and the betting table meets.*

*Write this once, as an agreement. It exists because cool-down is the first thing that gets taken, and it is easier to defend something the team wrote down than something everyone assumed.*

---

## 1. What cool-down is for

*Three things the book gives it. Keep or cut, but say which.*

- **Unscheduled work by choice.** *Bugs, small improvements, refactors. Chosen by whoever does them, not assigned.*
- **Exploration.** *Trying things, including things that will not become projects.*
- **The betting table.** *Shaping and betting happen here because the end of a cycle is the worst time to plan. Everyone is busy shipping.*

---

## 2. What cool-down is not

*The failure mode is specific and near-universal: cool-down becomes a place to park work that did not fit in the cycle. State the line explicitly, because it will be tested.*

- *Not overflow. Unfinished cycle work goes back to shaping, not into cool-down.*
- *Not a bug sprint with a list someone else wrote.*
- *Not the place to start the next cycle early.*

**One exception, and it is in the book.** Cool-down absorbs small overruns: a project that needs two more days to land can take them. What it does not absorb is a project that was not shaped well enough to finish, and the difference is whether the remaining work is downhill.

## 4. Shaping review

*This section is an addition. The method prescribes no meeting and no retrospective for cool-down. Delete it if you do not want one.*

*The reason to consider it: the book says that a project missing its cycle "means we did something wrong in the shaping." That is a diagnosis, and a diagnosis with no venue never gets made. Every cycle then repeats the same shaping mistake and blames the deadline.*

*Keep it to what shipped and what the shaping got wrong. Not team process, not morale, not a list of actions.*

**Per project that ran last cycle:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Shipped?** | *Yes / cut down and shipped / did not ship* |
| **What the pitch missed** | *Rabbit holes not called out, no-gos that were not enforced, an appetite that never fit.* |
| **What got hammered** | *What was cut, and whether it should have been a no-go from the start.* |

*One shared question: is anything showing up across projects? A rabbit hole that keeps recurring is usually a shaping habit, not bad luck.*

## Notes on using this template

*Delete this section too.*

**This is the document to write first, before you run a single cycle.** Cool-down is the part of the bargain that costs something visible and delivers nothing visible, which is exactly why it disappears. Teams that abandoned Shape Up describe the same decay: cool-down became a parking lot for everything that was not a main project, and then it became another cycle.

**Two weeks is not padding.** Shaping and betting for the next cycle happen in it. Cut cool-down and you have moved shaping into the cycle it was meant to precede.

**Do not turn the shaping review into a retrospective.** The book prescribes none, and adding a general process retro on top of an already opinionated method usually produces actions nobody owns. Section 4 asks one thing: did the shaping hold. That question has an answer.

**If cool-down keeps getting eaten, the problem is upstream.** Habitual overrun points at shaping or at capacity, and protecting cool-down harder does not fix either. Look at what is producing the overflow.

**Where this lives:** a wiki. It is an agreement about how the team works, it is read by people outside the team when they want to interrupt it, and it does not version with code.

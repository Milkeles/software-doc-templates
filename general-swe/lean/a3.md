# A3: {Issue}

*Also called: A3 report, A3 problem-solving.*

*Italic text is guidance. Delete it as you fill each section in.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Owner** | *One person. The A3 belongs to whoever is doing the thinking, not to a committee.* |
| **Coach** | *The person asking the questions. Optional, but the format assumes one.* |
| **Started** | YYYY-MM-DD |
| **Status** | *Drafting / Circulating / Agreed / Implementing / Closed* |

*One sheet. A3 or 11 by 17 inches. The size is the constraint and the constraint is the point: it is the largest sheet a group can read together around a table and the smallest that forces you to cut.*

*Sections 1 to 4 go down the left side of the sheet. Sections 5 to 8 go down the right. Left is what is true. Right is what to do about it. A reader can see from across the room whether you spent your effort on understanding or on arguing.*

*Circulate this half-finished. That is not a shortcut, it is the method. The draft is what the conversation happens around.*

---

# Left side: what is true

## 1. Issue

*A descriptive title. One line.*

*Name the problem, not the solution. "Deploys fail on Fridays" is an issue. "Move to blue-green deploys" is a proposal wearing an issue's clothes, and everything below it will be written to justify a conclusion you already reached.*

---

## 2. Background

*Why this matters and why now. Enough for someone outside the team to see where it sits.*

*Three or four lines. Include what it costs today and who feels it. A background section with no cost in it gives a reader no way to judge whether the rest is worth reading.*

> **Example.** Release deploys have failed on 6 of the last 14 attempts, all after 15:00. Each failure costs roughly two hours across three people, and twice we have shipped on Monday instead. Support absorbs the delay.

---

## 3. Current condition

*How the process actually works today, drawn, with the problems marked on the drawing and numbers attached to them.*

*Draw it. Boxes and arrows for the real sequence, not the documented one. Mark where the problem shows up. Put a number next to each mark.*

*This is the section that separates a real A3 from a proposal. It is also the one people rush. Go and look at the actual process before writing it. If you are describing it from memory, you are describing the process as it was designed rather than as it runs.*

```
 Merge ---> CI (12 min) ---> Manual QA ---> Deploy ---> Smoke
                                 |             |
                            [1] waits         [2] 6/14 fail
                            avg 3.5h          all after 15:00
```

*Then, briefly, what the data says. Counts and times, not adjectives.*

---

## 4. Cause analysis

*The chain of cause and effect from the problem back to something you can change.*

*Keep asking why until you reach something within reach. Stop when the next "why" leaves your control, and say that you stopped there.*

*Distinguish causes you have evidence for from causes you suspect. Marking a link as unconfirmed is more useful than asserting it, because it tells the reader what to check.*

> **Example.**
> Deploys fail after 15:00
> because the deploy runs against a database mid-backup
> because the backup window was moved to 15:00 in March
> because the backup was competing with the morning batch job.

*A cause chain that terminates at a person is usually incomplete. Keep going until it reaches a condition.*

---

# Right side: what to do

## 5. Target condition

*How the process will work once this is fixed, drawn the same way, plus what you predict the numbers will be.*

*Draw the new flow, not a list of improvements. Predicting a number here is what makes section 7 possible, so give one even if you are unsure. A prediction you got wrong is information. No prediction is not.*

> **Example.** Deploy waits for a backup lock rather than failing on it. Predicted: 0 backup-related failures, deploy time up by at most 5 minutes.

---

## 6. Implementation plan

*What has to happen, who does it, and when.*

| Action | Owner | By |
|---|---|---|
| | | |

*Names, not teams. A row owned by "Platform" is a row owned by nobody.*

---

## 7. Follow-up plan

*How and when you will check that the target condition was reached and the predicted numbers appeared.*

*State the date and the measure now, before you know the answer. A follow-up plan written after the fact selects for the measures that make the change look good.*

> **Example.** Review after 10 deploys or 3 weeks, whichever is first. Measure: backup-related deploy failures, and median deploy duration.

---

## 8. Results

**Leave this blank when you first circulate the A3.**

*An A3 that arrives complete has skipped the process it exists to impose. This box gets filled in after the follow-up date, with what actually happened.*

*Record the prediction miss, if there was one. That is the part with value in it.*

## Notes on using this template

*Delete this section too.*

**The section list is a convention, not a standard, and published versions disagree.** Some use these eight boxes, some seven with a separate Goal, some as many as ten. This template uses the eight that are documented in wording anyone can check. If your organisation already uses a different set, use theirs and stop worrying about it — the boxes matter far less than filling them in order.

**Do not skip a step to save time.** The order is the method: every box constrains the next one, and the tempting ones to drop are the analysis boxes that stop you writing down the solution you had already decided on. Where anyone has looked at this, people who worked through every step got consistent results and people who skipped even one mostly did not.

**The A3 is a conversation, not a report.** The draft gets passed around and discussed face to face while it is unfinished, and the sheet is what the discussion points at. The Lean Enterprise Institute's own definition is worth having: it is "much more than a sheet a paper with facts and figures. It is a management process learned through dialogue about concrete problems." An A3 written alone and presented finished has produced a document and none of the thing the document was for.

**One person owns it.** The format assumes an owner doing the thinking and a coach asking questions, and it collapses into a status report without both.

**Where this lives:** on paper while it is alive. Photograph it into the wiki when it closes, along with what section 8 ended up saying. The photograph is the record; the sheet was the work.

*The Scrum Guide defines this as three things: the Sprint Goal (why), the selected Product Backlog items (what), and an actionable plan for delivering the Increment (how). Most teams produce the second and skip the first, which is where Sprints start failing.*

*This belongs to the Developers. They update it daily. Nobody else changes it.*

---

## Sprint Goal

*One sentence. The single objective of this Sprint, stated so a stakeholder understands why the Sprint is worth running.*

*Crafted by the whole Scrum Team in Sprint Planning and finalised before Planning ends.*

**The test that matters:** *could you drop one of the selected items and still achieve this Goal?*

*If not, your Goal is a summary of the item list, and you have given away the mechanism the Sprint depends on. The Guide allows Developers to renegotiate scope with the Product Owner mid-Sprint "without affecting the Sprint Goal". That only works when the Goal is an outcome and the items are one route to it. A team whose Goal is "finish these six items" has nothing to renegotiate, so every surprise becomes a failed Sprint.*

> **Weak.** Complete the export API, the CSV formatter and the download page.
>
> **Better.** Merchants can export their transaction history themselves, so support stops running exports by hand.

*Write it as the outcome someone outside the team would notice.*

*The Product Backlog items pulled in to achieve the Goal. Keep this list in the tracker, not here; this template describes what it should contain.*

*Mark which items are essential to the Goal and which are the flexible part. Doing this at Planning, rather than in week two under pressure, is what makes mid-Sprint renegotiation a decision instead of an argument.*

| Item | Essential to the Goal | Size |
|---|---|---|
| | *Yes / No* | |

*On sizing: the Guide assigns it to "the Developers who will be doing the work", and in 2020 replaced the word "estimate" with "size" throughout. Use whatever unit tells you an item is too big to finish. That is the only judgement sizing has to support.*

## The plan

*How the Developers intend to deliver the Increment. Enough detail to inspect progress at the Daily Scrum, and no more.*

*The Guide does not require a task breakdown. It says decomposing items into pieces of a day or less is what teams "often" do, then states that "how this is done is at the sole discretion of the Developers". The word "task" does not appear in the 2020 Guide at all. If your tracker forces subtasks, that is the tracker's opinion.*

*What the plan must support is one question each day: are we still going to meet the Goal? Anything that helps answer it belongs; anything that only produces a nicer burndown does not.*

**Order and dependencies.** *What has to happen before what, and what can run in parallel.*

**Risks to the Goal.** *What could stop this Sprint achieving its objective, and what you would do about each. Name them at Planning while there is time to act.*

| Risk | If it happens |
|---|---|
| *The payments sandbox is unavailable again* | *Build against the recorded fixtures, accept a manual verification before release* |

## Notes on using this template

*Delete this section too.*

**Live in the tracker, not in a document.** The Sprint Backlog changes daily and belongs where the work is. Use this template to check that yours contains all three parts, then keep it there.

**Write the Goal before selecting items, not after.** Sprint Planning Topic One is "Why is this Sprint valuable?" and it comes first for a reason. A Goal reverse-engineered from a chosen list will always be a summary of that list, which is the failure this whole document is arranged to prevent.

**A Sprint with no coherent Goal is a signal, not a formatting problem.** If the work genuinely has no single objective, either the backlog is not ordered against the [Product Goal](product-goal.md), or the team is absorbing unrelated demand and should say so. Inventing a Goal to fill the field hides both.

**Where this lives:** the tracker.

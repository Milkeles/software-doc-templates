# Business plan: {Game or studio}

*Also called: a pitch deck, or a greenlight document when prepared for a publisher.*

*Italic text is guidance. Delete it as you fill each section in.*

| **Ask** | *One line: what you want the reader to do* |

*Industry accounts of publisher pitching converge on one operating fact worth designing this whole document around: a first-pass pitch review gets very little of the reader's time. Lead with the ask and the pitch, in that order, and put supporting detail after. A reader who stops after the first page should still know what you want and whether it interests them.*

## 1. The pitch

*What the [game design document](game-design-document.md)'s overview says, compressed further. One paragraph. If a reader cannot repeat your game back to a colleague after this paragraph, it is not short enough yet.*

---

## 2. Market and comparables

*Games this sits next to, and what that tells a reader about audience size and expectation. Not an exhaustive market analysis; three or four honest comparables a reader already knows.*

| Comparable | Similar because | Different because |
|---|---|---|
| | | |

*State your position plainly: same genre and smaller scope, same audience and a new mechanic, adjacent genre with a specific gap. A pitch that will not say what it is like reads as either naive about its own market or unwilling to be judged against it.*

---

## 3. Audience

*Who buys this, specifically enough to be checked. "Everyone who likes fun games" tells a publisher nothing they can plan against; "players of X and Y, 18-34, who have shown willingness to pay for cosmetic content" does.*

## 5. Budget and milestones

*Publisher funding in this industry is typically milestone-based: payments released against named, dated deliverables, with the publisher's share of revenue weighted in their favour until their advance is recouped, after which the split moves toward the developer. Structure this section so a reader can check your deliverables against your ask.*

| Milestone | Deliverable | Target date | Funding released | Recoupment status after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Vertical slice* | *One level, full-quality, representative of final game* | | | |
| *Alpha* | *Feature-complete, unpolished* | | | |
| *Beta* | *Content-complete* | | | |
| *Gold* | *Certified and submitted* | | | |

*Do not promise a milestone date your team has not actually estimated. A missed milestone against a funding agreement is a worse outcome than a longer, honest schedule pitched up front.*

---

## 6. Team

*Who is building this and what they have shipped before. A publisher is funding a team's ability to finish as much as a game's ability to sell.*

## 7. Risks

*The risks a sophisticated reader will think of whether you name them or not: technical risk, scope risk, key-person risk, platform or certification risk, competitive risk. Naming them yourself, with a mitigation, reads as competence. Leaving them for the reader to find reads as either not having considered them or hoping nobody asks.*

## Notes on using this template

*Delete this section too.*

**This is not the game design document, and should not try to be.** A publisher does not need the full mechanics and dynamics sections; they need the pitch, the audience, and the money. Link the game design document for a reader who wants the design depth rather than reproducing it here.

**This is not the art bible, and should not try to be.** Include only the concept art that sells the pitch, not the production standard. A publisher deciding whether to fund you is not the audience for a polycount table.

**Lead with the ask.** A pitch document that saves its terms for the last page assumes a reader who will get there. Reviewers spending a couple of minutes on a first pass will not.

**Name your risks before your reader finds them.** An unlisted risk that a reviewer spots on their own costs you more credibility than the same risk named with a mitigation attached.

**Where this lives:** wherever the pitch process already lives, wiki, shared drive, or a deck tool, not the source repository. Its readers are frequently outside the company and do not use one.
